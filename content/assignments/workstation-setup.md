## The task

Set up a workstation properly, document it with photos, and complete a checklist against the neutral posture standard. Then identify what you cannot fix and what you will do about it.

Do this at whichever workstation you use most: home, school, or a laptop on a kitchen table. Nobody has a perfect setup, and a realistic one that you actually improved is worth more than a photo of an ideal desk you do not sit at.

## Before you start

- [Ergonomics and the Workstation](/learn/computing/ergonomics-and-the-workstation/) (`COMP-110`) — neutral posture, adjustment sequence, and viewing distance
- [What a Computer Is Doing](/learn/computing/what-a-computer-is-doing/) (`COMP-101`) — core components, data pipeline, and hardware limits

## Steps

{{% steps %}}

### Step 1 — Adjust in order

Order matters. Good equipment set up in the wrong sequence still hurts. Take your time with each one in order: chair, then keyboard and mouse, then monitor. If a chair or desk does not adjust, note it and move on.

### Step 2 — Measure two things

Use a tape measure, a ruler, or a phone measuring app:

1. **Eye to screen distance**: Measure in inches from your eyes to the screen surface (target: 20 to 40 inches).
2. **Screen height**: Check whether the top of the screen sits at or slightly below your eye level.

### Step 3 — Take three photos

Document your workstation:

- **Side view, seated and working**: This is the important one. Have someone take it, or prop your phone and use a timer. Capture your whole body from the side, showing your back, arms, and feet.
- **Your monitor and desk**: Showing the screen position, viewing distance, and keyboard/mouse placement.
- **Your feet and chair**: Showing whether your feet rest flat on the floor or footrest and your thighs are level.

> *You do not need to show your face. Frame it however you are comfortable, and remember your portfolio is public.*

### Step 4 — Complete the checklist

Answer **Yes** or **No** to each item. For every "No," write a brief sentence explaining what stops you (e.g., non-adjustable desk, chair too high, fixed monitor stand):

1. Feet flat on the floor or on a footrest
2. Thighs roughly parallel to the floor
3. Lower back supported by lumbar support or backrest
4. Shoulders relaxed, elbows between 90 and 120 degrees
5. Wrists straight while typing, not bent up or down
6. Mouse at the same height as the keyboard
7. Monitor directly in front, not off to one side
8. Top of screen at or slightly below eye level
9. Screen between 20 and 40 inches from your eyes
10. No glare or bright window directly behind the screen

### Step 5 — Fix one thing

Choose your worst "No" from the checklist and fix it now, even roughly.

Improvised fixes count: a stack of textbooks under a monitor, a folded towel behind your back for lumbar support, or a sturdy box under your feet as a footrest. Photograph the **before** and **after**.

### Step 6 — Write a break plan

Write a 1-to-2 sentence routine for how you will build movement and eye relief into long work sessions:

- How you will remember the **20-20-20 rule** (look at an object 20 feet away for 20 seconds every 20 minutes)
- How you will take micro-breaks to stand up and stretch every 20 to 30 minutes

Pick a method you will actually use (a phone timer, an OS notification, or a recurring habit trigger).

### Step 7 — Post to your portfolio

On your portfolio's **Coursework** page, add a post using the standard write-up format:

- **Your three setup photos** (side view, desk/monitor, feet/chair)
- **Your completed 10-point checklist** with concrete reasons for every "No"
- **Your before-and-after fix photos** and an explanation of what you improved
- **Your break plan**

## Notes

- **Improvised fixes count**: A stack of textbooks under a monitor or a box under your feet is a legitimate solution and will earn full credit. This is not an assignment about owning expensive furniture.
- **Laptops cannot be fixed by posture alone**: If the screen is at the right height the keyboard is too high, and the reverse. Say so in your checklist rather than pretending otherwise, and note what you would need to solve it (such as an external keyboard and mouse).
- **You will come back to this**: In Game Art & Design 1 you will be in the lab five hours a week. Setting up the station you sit at becomes a habit, and the first lab of that course starts with it.
