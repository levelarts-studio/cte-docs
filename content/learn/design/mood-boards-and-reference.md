## Read

### Nobody works from memory

Professional concept and prop artists gather reference before touching their tool, every time, without exception. This isn't a beginner habit you outgrow. It's how the work actually gets made, because your memory of what a hinge or a vent or a weathered handle looks like is vaguer and less accurate than an actual photo of one, no matter how many you've seen.

A reference board is a single organized place holding everything you're pulling from for one piece: photos, other artists' work, color, material, whatever informs the decisions you're about to make. Built before modeling starts, and kept open the whole time you work.

### Three layers, not one pile

A board that's just forty images dumped in a grid doesn't help you as much as one that's organized by what each image is actually informing. Three useful categories:

- **Primary form**: The big, overall silhouette. What is this object's basic shape and proportion, before any detail exists. If your prop is a lantern, this is photos showing the overall lantern shape from a few angles.
- **Secondary form**: The features sitting on top of the primary shape: handles, vents, seams, panel lines, hinges, ridges. These are the details that make a shape specific rather than generic.
- **Material callouts**: What things are actually made of, and how that material behaves. Worn brass looks different from painted steel, which looks different from scratched plastic. This is the layer people skip and it's the layer that makes a render convincing.

Group your board into these three zones, roughly, even if the boundaries blur a little. A board organized this way answers a specific question at a glance instead of making you scroll through everything every time.

### Building your board in Canva

You already have a Canva account, so that's the tool for this class. Open a blank design, pick a large canvas (a "Whiteboard" template works well since it isn't locked to a fixed page shape), and build three labeled zones: **Primary Form**, **Secondary Form**, and **Material**.

Drag images into each zone. Canva can pull directly from a web image search inside the editor, or you can upload photos you've saved. Resize freely, and add a text box under each material image with your callout.

That's the whole workflow, and it's enough to do everything this assignment asks for.

### The tool the industry actually uses

Once you're gathering reference constantly, for every project, you'll want something built specifically for it. That tool is **PureRef**, free, and standard across the industry from students to professional studios.

What it does that a slide tool doesn't:

- **Infinite canvas**: Never runs out of room, at any zoom level.
- **Drag anything in, instantly**: From a folder, browser, or screenshot.
- **Always on top**: The board floats above your other windows, so you glance at reference without alt-tabbing away from Blender.
- **One file**: A `.pur` file embeds every image, so the whole board travels as a single file.

That last point, floating above your modeling software while you work, is the thing a slide-based board can't do. It's worth installing once you're building reference boards regularly, and it's free.

This unit, use Canva. If you want to try PureRef, it's a quick install and I'm glad to show you.

Studying how working production artists set up their boards shows how research directly influences 3D asset quality:

### Gathering with intent

Random image searches produce a random board. A few habits that produce a useful one, whichever tool you use:

- **Search specifically**: Not "lantern," but "brass camping lantern hinge detail" or "lantern handle wear." The more specific the search, the more useful the result.
- **Get multiple angles of the same object where you can**: A three-quarter view alone hides how a form actually reads from the side.
- **Include at least one real photo for every material you plan to use**: Concept art is useful for form; photos are what tell you the truth about how a material actually catches light.
- **Keep gathering references while you work, not just before**: If you hit a problem mid-model, that's a reason to add reference, not push through blind.

### Reading a reference like an artist

Look past what an object is and notice how it's built. A lantern's handle isn't just "a handle": is it a simple bent rod, or does it have a wrapped grip? Does the body taper or stay a constant width? Where exactly does the glass panel meet the frame? Those are the questions your primary and secondary form reference should be answering before you open Blender.
