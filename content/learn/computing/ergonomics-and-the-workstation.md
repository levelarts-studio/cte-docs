## Read

### Why this comes first

You are about to spend three years at a computer, and the creative industries run long hours in front of screens. Injuries from bad setups build slowly and then stick around. Nothing about this is dramatic; it is just much easier to set up correctly now than to fix a wrist problem at 25.

### Neutral posture

OSHA defines neutral posture as the position where stress on your muscles, tendons, and skeleton is lowest. Concretely:

- Head level, facing forward, balanced over your spine
- Shoulders relaxed, upper arms hanging naturally at your sides
- Elbows close to the body, bent between 90 and 120 degrees
- Wrists and forearms straight, in line, roughly parallel to the floor
- Feet flat on the floor, thighs roughly parallel to the floor

Every adjustment below exists to put one body part into that position.

### Adjust in this order

Order matters. Good equipment set up in the wrong sequence still hurts.

1. **Chair first.** Set the height so your feet rest flat and your thighs are parallel to the floor. Leave a two- to three-finger gap behind your knees. Get your lower back against the lumbar support.  
   If your feet do not reach, use a footrest angled no more than 30 degrees. Do not use the chair base as a footrest.

2. **Keyboard and mouse to your elbows.** The keyboard sits at or just below elbow height, close to you, flat or tilted slightly away. Wrists straight, not bent up, down, or sideways.  
   The mouse goes at the same height and on the same plane as the keyboard. Move it with your whole arm rather than pivoting at the wrist. Twisting the wrist side to side, hundreds of times an hour, is where trouble starts.

3. **Monitor to your eyes.** Directly in front of you, so you look straight ahead without tilting your head forward or back. Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level.  
   Distance matters more than people expect. OSHA gives a preferred viewing range of 20 to 40 inches from your eye to the screen surface. Too close and your eyes work harder to focus, and you end up leaning back and typing with outstretched arms.  
   If text is hard to read at that distance, increase the text size. Do not move the monitor closer.

### Your eyes

You blink less when looking at a screen, which is why eyes get dry and tired. The fix is deliberate: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet away for 20 seconds. Blink on purpose while you do it.

Watch glare, too. A window directly behind you puts reflections on the screen; a window directly behind the monitor puts you looking into bright light all day. Sideways to a window is best.

### The best posture is the next one

No position is good for three hours. Even a perfect setup gets uncomfortable if you do not move.

Stand up, stretch, or walk around every 20 to 30 minutes. In a two-and-a-half-hour lab, that is a few short breaks, and you will get more done, not less.

### Working on a laptop

A laptop cannot be ergonomic on its own: if the screen is at the right height, the keyboard is too high, and vice versa. If you work on a laptop a lot, an external keyboard and mouse plus something to raise the screen fixes it cheaply. A stack of books works.
